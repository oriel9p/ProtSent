Residue-level MaxSim for protein retrieval

A dense protein model squeezes each protein into one vector and compares two proteins with a single dot product. A late-interaction model keeps one small vector per residue, and compares two proteins by letting every residue of one shop for its best match in the other and adding those matches up — that operation is MaxSim. This is what that costs, what it buys, and which of the gains need training at all. Measured on A100-80GBs with sentence-transformers 6.0.

The short version

Late interaction is worth having. Whether you need to train for it depends entirely on the backbone.

Swapping pooled cosine for residue MaxSim improves retrieval on both backbones with zero training. Late training then helps both backbones too — but the 64-D projection it trains through is not always where the gain lives. On vanilla ESM-2 the head is the trained space and beats the backbone's own residues by +.077 MAP. On ProtSent-V2, whose residues were already contrastively trained by its 34.8M-pair run, the same head discards .026 MAP — more than the training added. Score that trained backbone at its native 480-D instead and it is the best configuration in the pilot. The ordering survives restriction to low-identity queries; alignment still leads at rank 1 while the late arms win ranking depth.

Every figure on these four cards is superfamily-level eligible MAP on SCOPe-40. Tables further down report family level too, which is what earlier ProtSent work published — check the block header before comparing a card to a row.

No masked-language-model pretraining happened anywhere, and no per-task fine-tuning happened anywhere. Both trained arms are a short contrastive (sentence-transformer) continuation from an existing checkpoint, on the general ProtSent-V2 pair corpus — never on a benchmark's own training set.

Terms used throughout

MaxSim
the late-interaction score: for each residue of the query, its best match anywhere in the document, summed. One number per protein pair, as cosine gives one number per pair.
R@1 / R@10
recall at 1 and 10: the fraction of queries with at least one correct hit that high in the ranking. MAP is mean average precision over the whole ranking, so it rewards depth that R@10 cannot see.
Eligible
a query with at least one non-self protein sharing its label. The rest are unreachable for any method, so they are excluded from the headline metrics (1,693 / 2,020 / 2,116 queries at family / superfamily / fold).
Transfer suite
ProtBench's 13-task benchmark of frozen-embedding probes — everything from solubility to secondary structure. Distinct from "mean-pooled", which is what the model does to residues.
W / T / L
win / tie / loss counts across those tasks, ties being |Δ| < 0.005.
EAT
embedding-based annotation transfer: label a query with its nearest neighbour's label. The CATH convention, and the protocol behind that benchmark's published numbers.

Method, in full

Head
token-level Dense(480→64, no bias, no activation) — 30,720 weights, the entire trained addition → mask → per-residue L2 normalisation. Randomly initialised; step 0 is therefore a random projection.
Masking
<cls> and <eos> excluded from scoring on both sides. There is no ColBERT-style query expansion and no query/document prefix: the two encoders are literally the same function, so encode_query ≡ encode_document.
Loss
CachedMultiVectorMultipleNegativesRankingLoss, scale 1.0 (correct for unnormalised MaxSim; the dense recipe's 20.0 would be wrong), in-batch negatives only — no hard-negative mining.
Optimisation
backbone LR 1e-5, fresh projection LR 1e-4, cosine schedule, 100 warmup steps, bf16, max_seq_length 512, seed 42.
Data
round-robin over protsent-data-dc40/{pfam_sorted, afdb_sorted, stringdb_train_15M}.parquet, built into 777k + 2.0M + 2.0M pairs (AFDB and STRING capped at 2M each, taken from the group-sorted prefix). Effective contrastive batch 256 = 2 GPUs × 128, gather_across_devices off, so in-batch negatives are 128.
Control
the step-0 dense view reproduces the base model's pooled embeddings to 0.0 max absolute difference on both backbones — the wrapper adds nothing and removes nothing.

What it costs, explained plainly

A dense model boils each protein down to one 480-number summary; two proteins are compared with a single dot product. A late-interaction model keeps one small vector per residue, and compares two proteins by letting every residue of one shop for its best match in the other, then adding those matches up (that is MaxSim). Same reading of the protein, much richer comparison. The extra cost lands in exactly two places: storage and scoring.

Two things follow that matter beyond a benchmark. Storage scales with residues: at UniRef50 scale (~60M sequences, mean length ~350, fp16) a 64-D late index is ≈2.7 TB against ≈58 GB dense, and the native 480-D variant is 7.5× larger again. Scoring scales with pairs, so nobody deploys brute-force MaxSim — the two-stage alternative is priced in the SCOPe section below.

The transfer suite never touches MaxSim. Late-trained models are evaluated there through their dense view — the same backbone with the original mean-pooling head — so those numbers stay directly comparable to every previous ProtSent run and cost exactly what a dense model costs.

Every timing on this page leaves ~2× on the table

None of these runs used flash attention. The shared environment ships kernels 0.12.3, below the 0.15.2 floor the flash kernels need, and flash-attn is absent — so every run silently fell back to sdpa. In an isolated venv with attn_implementation="kernels-community/vllm-flash-attn3" and bf16, the same ProtSent-V2-35M training step measures 240.9 pairs/s against 122.2 on sdpa (1.97×) on Pfam, and 135.6 against 91.4 (1.48×) on STRING, with peak VRAM falling 10.7 → 4.2 GB. Numerics check out (pooled cosine agreement ≥ .99974, MaxSim top-1 retrieval agreement 100%), though FA3-produced and sdpa-produced eval numbers should not be mixed in one table — nothing in this report's results tables came from that environment. Three corollaries. The ~21% late-vs-dense training overhead above is an sdpa-vs-sdpa figure and is smaller than the attention backend choice in either direction. The win is mostly not the kernel: forcing unpadding off under flash gives 104.0 pairs/s against 135.6 with it on, so the 1.48× splits into ~1.30× unpadding and ~1.14× kernel — and since Sentence Transformers enables unpadding automatically whenever flash is active, the earlier token-packing null result is explained (there is no padding left for packing to reclaim; what packing does buy is memory predictability, 8.49 → 7.66 GB peak). Finally torch.compile is length-dependent rather than uniformly bad — +3.6% on Pfam, −13% on the longer STRING sequences — but ~250 s of warmup needs ~13k steps to break even either way, so it stays off at this scale. Untested at 150M.

One caveat before this reaches a training recipe: the current setup keeps fp32 master weights and autocasts, while the flash path wants bf16 weights, which moves the optimiser states with it. fp16 is not an option at all here — it fails outright with "Attempting to unscale FP16 gradients". Speed this large is worth having, but retrieval quality on a real run should be checked before the recipe changes, not after.

Where the gain actually lives

Every earlier revision of this report confounded two things: what late training does to the backbone, and what the 64-D projection does to the scores. They separate cleanly by scoring each trained backbone twice — once through its head, once over its own 480-D residues. So the 64-D head is not universally lossy, and late training never degraded anything. The projection is load-bearing when the backbone's residues have never been contrastively trained — it is the space the objective actually optimised — and lossy when they have, because it bottlenecks representations that 34.8M pairs of training had already shaped. That is why the same recipe reads as a triumph from vanilla ESM-2 and as a wash from ProtSent-V2, and it makes the head-size question the pilot's most valuable remaining knob for one init and irrelevant for the other.

It also names the best configuration measured here, which no earlier revision contained: ProtSent-V2 + late training, scored at native 480-D — superfamily eligible MAP .698, against .683 for the untouched backbone and .672 for the shipped 64-D arm. It costs 1.5 GPU-h and a 154× index rather than a 20.6× one.

How fast the training saturates

SCOPe superfamily MAP scored at every checkpoint. Both arms converge to the same place and are essentially flat after ~1,500 steps. The runs consumed 512k of the 4.8M pairs built, so this is not exhaustion — but the pair build took the group-sorted prefix of AFDB and STRING, so the training pairs came from a narrow slice of clusters. Diversity, not volume, is the open question.

One thing we did not expect

Query and document are encoded by the identical function here, so it is tempting to symmetrise the score matrix. Doing it naively destroys the ranking: a raw MaxSim row's scale is the query's length, so averaging with the transpose smuggles document length into the comparison. Length-normalising (MeanMaxSim) stops the collapse but still costs every arm except the untouched ProtSent-V2, and costs the two trained arms most — −.275 and −.128 MAP:

 The directionality carries real signal — a short domain matching inside a long protein is a genuine asymmetric relation, and averaging it away discards that. Trained arms rely on it more than untrained ones, which is itself evidence that the contrastive objective's query/document roles shape the representation even when both roles run the same encoder. Practical rule: score in one direction and keep it consistent.

What this does not yet show

  • One seed, one run per arm. The SCOPe comparisons carry paired bootstrap CIs over queries; the transfer tables carry none at all. With a 0.005 tie threshold, read the tallies and the sign changes, not the third decimal.
  • Neither retrieval benchmark is decontaminated against the pretraining corpus. The identity stratification above is the mitigation, not a clean split — the median SCOPe domain sits at 0.91 max identity to training data.
  • The CATH table has two independent problems. Its 150 queries make one protein worth 0.67 points, so the small trained-vs-untrained gaps there are not resolvable; and its dense rows are published EAT numbers on a different scoring rule, so the large dense → MaxSim deltas are cross-protocol upper bounds. A single-pipeline rerun that makes every pair McNemar-testable is running. Alignment baselines also exist only at the family level, not at superfamily, which is the level declared primary here.
  • No hard-negative mining, one head size, one scale, one training corpus slice. In-batch negatives only; 64-D and 128-D but nothing larger; 35M with 150M still training; and AFDB/STRING pairs drawn from the group-sorted prefix of the corpus rather than sampled across it, so 2,000 steps saw a narrow slice of clusters. Hard negatives are the largest untouched lever.
  • Function coverage is thin, and this is V2 not V2.5. EC, metal-ion binding and catalytic efficiency are in; GO-MF and CAFA5 are not, so "does late training hurt function prediction?" is half-answered. The pilot also builds on ProtSent-V2 while the shipped small model is V2.5, so none of these numbers should be quoted against V2.5 results. There is also no comparison against the PROTOCOL benchmark — different splits and relevance definitions.
