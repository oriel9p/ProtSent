Residue-level MaxSim for protein retrieval

What ColBERT-style late interaction costs, what it buys, and which of those gains need training at all. Everything here is measured on this box — A100-80GBs, sentence-transformers 6.0, a 2,000-step contrastive continuation per arm. Fourth revision: scoring the trained backbones at their native 480-D residues, rather than through the 64-D head, separated two effects that were confounded in every earlier version — and reversed the conclusion about what late training does to a ProtSent backbone.

The short version

Late interaction is worth having. Whether you need to train for it depends entirely on the backbone.

Swapping pooled cosine for residue MaxSim improves retrieval on both backbones with zero training. Late training then helps both backbones too — but the 64-D projection it trains through is not always where the gain lives. On vanilla ESM-2 the head is the trained space and beats the backbone's own residues by +.077 MAP. On ProtSent-V2, whose residues were already contrastively trained by its 34.8M-pair run, the same head discards .026 MAP — more than the training added. Score that trained backbone at its native 480-D instead and it is the best configuration in the pilot. The ordering survives restriction to low-identity queries; alignment still leads at rank 1 while the late arms win ranking depth.

What was trained, from what

No masked-language-model pretraining happened anywhere, and no per-task fine-tuning happened anywhere. Both trained arms are a short contrastive (sentence-transformer) continuation from an existing checkpoint, on the general ProtSent-V2 pair corpus — never on a benchmark's own training set.

Method, in full

Head
token-level Dense(480→64, no bias, no activation) → mask → per-residue L2 normalisation. Randomly initialised; step 0 is therefore a random projection.
Masking
<cls> and <eos> excluded from scoring on both sides. There is no ColBERT-style query expansion and no query/document prefix: the two encoders are literally the same function, so encode_query ≡ encode_document.
Loss
CachedMultiVectorMultipleNegativesRankingLoss, scale 1.0 (correct for unnormalised MaxSim; the dense recipe's 20.0 would be wrong), in-batch negatives only — no hard-negative mining.
Optimisation
backbone LR 1e-5, fresh projection LR 1e-4, cosine schedule, 100 warmup steps, bf16, max_seq_length 512, seed 42.
Data
round-robin over protsent-data-dc40/{pfam_sorted, afdb_sorted, stringdb_train_15M}.parquet, built into 777k + 2.0M + 2.0M pairs (AFDB and STRING capped at 2M each, taken from the group-sorted prefix). Effective contrastive batch 256 = 2 GPUs × 128, gather_across_devices off, so in-batch negatives are 128.
Control
the step-0 dense view reproduces the base model's pooled embeddings to 0.0 max absolute difference on both backbones — the wrapper adds nothing and removes nothing.

What it costs, explained plainly

A dense model boils each protein down to one 480-number summary; two proteins are compared with a single dot product. A late-interaction model keeps one small vector per residue, and compares two proteins by letting every residue of one shop for its best match in the other, then adding those matches up (that is MaxSim). Same reading of the protein, much richer comparison. The extra cost lands in exactly two places: storage and scoring.

Two things follow that matter for anything bigger than a benchmark. Storage scales with residues: at UniRef50 scale (~60M sequences, mean length ~350, fp16) a 64-D late index is ≈2.7 TB against ≈58 GB dense. Scoring scales with pairs, so nobody deploys brute-force MaxSim — the standard answer is two-stage retrieval, which this pilot priced without spending a GPU-minute:

Every timing on this page leaves ~2× on the table

None of these runs used flash attention. The shared environment ships kernels 0.12.3, below the 0.15.2 floor the flash kernels need, and flash-attn is absent — so every run silently fell back to sdpa. In an isolated venv with attn_implementation="kernels-community/vllm-flash-attn3" and bf16, the same ProtSent-V2-35M training step measures 240.9 pairs/s against 122.2 on sdpa (1.97×) on Pfam, and 135.6 against 91.4 (1.48×) on STRING, with peak VRAM falling 10.7 → 4.2 GB. Numerics check out (pooled cosine agreement ≥ .99974, MaxSim top-1 retrieval agreement 100%), though FA3-produced and sdpa-produced eval numbers should not be mixed in one table. Two corollaries: the ~21% late-vs-dense training overhead above is an sdpa-vs-sdpa figure and is smaller than the attention backend choice in either direction; and the token-packing null result reported earlier has a better explanation than "sdpa" — with flash attention Sentence Transformers already unpads inputs automatically, so packing has no padding left to reclaim (240.9 → 239.2 pairs/s) and buys memory predictability instead. torch.compile measured a net loss at this scale (−13% steady-state plus 266 s of warmup), consistent with the upstream note about small models on fast GPUs.

The 18-task pooled benchmark suite never touches MaxSim. Late-trained models are evaluated there through their dense view — the same backbone with the original mean-pooling head — so those numbers stay directly comparable to every previous ProtSent run and cost exactly what a dense model costs.

Where the gain actually lives

Every earlier revision of this report confounded two things: what late training does to the backbone, and what the 64-D projection does to the scores. They separate cleanly by scoring each trained backbone twice — once through its head, once over its own 480-D residues. Same weights, same queries, paired bootstrap over the 2,020 eligible superfamily queries.

So the 64-D head is not universally lossy, and late training never degraded anything. The projection is load-bearing when the backbone's residues have never been contrastively trained — it is the space the objective actually optimised — and lossy when they have, because it bottlenecks representations that 34.8M pairs of training had already shaped. That is why the same recipe reads as a triumph from vanilla ESM-2 and as a wash from ProtSent-V2, and it makes the head-size question the pilot's most valuable remaining knob for one init and irrelevant for the other.

It also names the best configuration measured here, which no earlier revision contained: ProtSent-V2 + late training, scored at native 480-D — superfamily eligible MAP .698, against .683 for the untouched backbone and .672 for the shipped 64-D arm. It costs 1.5 GPU-h and a 154× index rather than a 20.6× one.

Read that gap carefully: test_h holds 150 queries, so one protein is 0.67 points and 3.3 points is five proteins. Each arm's own 95% interval is ±8 points, and even the most favourable paired (McNemar) assumption — that the two arms disagree on exactly those five queries and no others — gives p = 0.07. Note CATH is encode-dominated (150 × 69,605), which is why 64-D is only 1.6× faster than 480-D here against 5× on SCOPe.

How fast the training saturates

SCOPe superfamily MAP scored at every checkpoint. Both arms converge to the same place and are essentially flat after ~1,500 steps. The runs consumed 512k of the 4.8M pairs built, so this is not exhaustion — but the pair build took the group-sorted prefix of AFDB and STRING, so the training pairs came from a narrow slice of clusters. Diversity, not volume, is the open question.

Did the backbone survive? It depends on the probe

The foundation-model question: after late training, is the ordinary mean-pooled 480-D embedding still good? Same protocol for every arm — ProtBench fast suite, test split, seed 42, 20k-sample cap — run twice, once with a KNN probe (k=3) and once with a linear probe. The two are reported side by side and never averaged, because they disagree, and the disagreement is the finding.

Five rows are excluded from the tallies because ProtBench forces a linear probe for them in both passes (EC classification and secondary structure are multilabel/token-level; the three SCOPe rows are retrieval, and are the benchmark the late head was trained to win anyway). One thing we did not expect

Query and document are encoded by the identical function here, so it is tempting to symmetrise the score matrix. Doing it naively destroys the ranking, because a raw MaxSim row's scale is the query's length, so averaging with the transpose smuggles document length into the comparison. Even the scale-free form (MeanMaxSim, each score divided by its query's residue count) costs the trained arms badly:

 The directionality carries real signal — a short domain matching inside a long protein is a genuine asymmetric relation, and averaging it away discards that. Trained arms rely on it more than untrained ones, which is itself evidence that the contrastive objective's query/document roles shape the representation even when both roles run the same encoder. Practical rule: score in one direction and keep it consistent.

What this does not yet show

  • One seed, one run per arm, no confidence intervals on the pooled tables. Both probes are now run and reported side by side, which is what exposed the Stability reversal — but with a single seed and a 0.005 tie threshold, individual small deltas are noise. Read the tallies and the sign changes, not the third decimal.
  • SCOPe-40 is not decontaminated against the pretraining corpus (see the identity-stratified check above, which is the mitigation, not a clean split).
  • 2,000 steps on a prefix-limited pair build. The plateau may be a diversity limit rather than a real ceiling; the 10,000-step uncapped runs will confound "more steps" with "more diverse pairs" unless the 2,000-step uncapped control also gets run.
  • No hard-negative mining. Upstream guidance calls it essential for competitive late-interaction models; this pilot used in-batch negatives only. Probably the largest untapped gain.
  • One head size (64-D), one scale (35M). The 128-D head ablation and both 150M arms are running. CATH suggests 64-D is genuinely too small on an already-contrastive backbone, which makes the head-size result the one to watch.
  • Function benchmarks are thin. EC, metal-ion binding and catalytic efficiency are in; GO-MF and CAFA5 are not, so "does late training hurt function prediction?" is half-answered. EC is mildly negative for both late arms under both probes; metal-ion binding is negative under KNN but positive for ProtSent under linear.
  • The CATH table has two separate problems, and they are different problems. First, test_h is 150 queries, so every accuracy carries a ±8-point interval and the 3.3-point trained-vs-zero-shot difference is five proteins (McNemar p ≥ 0.07 under the most favourable pairing). Second — and this one is not about sample size — the dense rows are the published EAT numbers: euclidean 1-NN on unnormalised embeddings, a different scoring rule and a different code path from the cosine/MaxSim rows beside them. So the large dense → MaxSim gaps (+14.0 for ESM-2, 21 proteins) are cross-protocol comparisons, and an unknown share of them belongs to cosine-vs-euclidean rather than to MaxSim. Treat +14.0 as an upper bound. A six-arm rerun through one pipeline, which makes every pair properly paired and McNemar-testable, is running.
  • Built on ProtSent-V2, not the shipped V2.5. Numbers here should not be quoted against V2.5 results.
  • No PROTOCOL comparison. Different splits, relevance definitions and hierarchy level — a direct number-to-number comparison would be wrong without redoing their protocol.
